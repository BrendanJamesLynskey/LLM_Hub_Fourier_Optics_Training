Fourier Optics for Training

The companion to Fourier Optics for Inference: could a Fourier-optical engine accelerate LLM training? Training adds a backward pass, weights that change every step and gradients that need their own precision. Three decks work it out: where transforms appear in a training step, with an Amdahl analysis from a published script; optical backpropagation and in-situ training; and optical engines inside a training cluster.

The headline so far. Training does not rescue the transform share. For a Hyena-2 model of Llama-3-8B's shape at 4,096 tokens, transforms are 0.21% of a forward pass and 0.15% of a training step, because the backward pass has as many FFTs as the forward while the matmuls double. With block-circulant weights the step share is 12.2% (Amdahl bound 1.14x), but a fifth of that work multiplies two data-dependent spectra (the weight gradient) and cannot sit on a static mask, which leaves 9.7%. And the masks themselves change every step: on the series' illustrative 2-megapixel, 1 kHz device, rewriting them takes 0.5–2 s per step, comparable to or longer than the digital step. All numbers: analysis/results.md.

Glossary: Concepts and Where They Are Explained

Every concept the series relies on, with a short explanation. Each entry links to the slides that explain it and, where the numbers exist, the section of analysis/results.md. Optics and inference concepts are explained in the Fourier Optics for Inference glossary and linked, not repeated.

The backward pass

The 6N rule
Training a dense model costs about 6 FLOPs per parameter per token: 2 for the forward pass and 4 for the backward (the activation gradient and the weight gradient), plus an attention term that grows with sequence length. The analysis reproduces it exactly for the transformer variant.Explained in: FOptTrn 01 · the 6N rule, reproduced · · results.md §1
The gradient of a convolution is a correlation
For y = h ∗ u, the input gradient is G correlated with h, IFFT(G·conj(H)), and the filter gradient is G correlated with u, IFFT(G·conj(U)). A backward long convolution is one more FFT and two more Fourier-domain products than the forward; the analysis checks the formulas against direct sums and finite differences.Explained in: FOptTrn 01 · the gradient of a convolution is a correlation · FOptTrn 01 · three 4f passes · · results.md §6
Weight-masked and data-by-data products
A Fourier-domain product whose second operand is a weight spectrum (forward H, backward conj(H)) can be a mask written once per optimizer step. One whose operands both depend on the sample (conj(U)·G for the weight gradient) needs a new mask for every sequence, and in practice stays digital.Explained in: FOptTrn 01 · weight-masked and data-by-data products · FOptTrn 03 · data-by-data products stay digital · · results.md §2
Static share and the training Amdahl bound
The fraction of a training step's FLOPs that are transforms plus weight-masked products: what an engine with once-per-step masks could take. 1/(1−share) bounds the step speed-up. For Hyena-2 at 4,096 tokens it is 0.14%; for block-circulant weights 9.7%.Explained in: FOptTrn 01 · the training-step Amdahl table · FOptTrn 01 · interactive Amdahl explorer · FOptTrn 03 · the static Amdahl bound · · results.md §2 · compare the inference bound
Mask rewrites per optimizer step
An inference engine writes its filter spectra once; a training engine must rewrite them after every optimizer step, for the forward and the backward pass. Processing the whole step layer by layer amortises each write over every sequence, but the frame budget still rivals the digital step time.Explained in: FOptTrn 01 · masks rewritten every step · FOptTrn 03 · masks change every step · FOptTrn 03 · interactive: step time against mask time · · results.md §4 · compare mask capacity

Training the optics

Diffractive deep neural networks (D²NN)
Stacks of passive diffractive layers whose transmission is trained in simulation and then fabricated (Lin et al., Science 2018). Training is entirely digital; the optics only runs inference, so model mismatch after fabrication is the central problem.Explained in: FOptTrn 02 · in-silico training and the reality gap
In-situ backpropagation (the adjoint method)
In a reciprocal photonic circuit, the gradient with respect to every phase shifter can be measured by sending the error signal backwards and interfering it with the stored forward field (Hughes et al., Optica 2018; demonstrated in a programmable photonic chip by Pai et al., Science 2023). The hardware computes its own gradient.Explained in: FOptTrn 02 · the adjoint method · FOptTrn 02 · in-situ backpropagation, demonstrated
Physics-aware training
Run the forward pass on the physical system and the backward pass through a differentiable digital model of it, so the update uses the real output but an approximate gradient (Wright et al., Nature 2022). It tolerates mismatch that pure in-silico training does not.Explained in: FOptTrn 02 · physics-aware training · FOptTrn 02 · interactive: three trainers
Direct feedback alignment (DFA)
Replace each layer's backpropagated error with a fixed random projection of the output error. The random projection is a large matrix multiply an optical scattering medium does for free; a photonic co-processor has trained networks this way (Launay et al., 2020). It scales poorly to transformers' accuracy.Explained in: FOptTrn 02 · direct feedback alignment
The reality gap
The difference between the model a network was trained on and the hardware it runs on: fabrication error, drift, crosstalk, temperature. In-situ and hardware-in-the-loop training close it; in-silico training does not.Explained in: FOptTrn 02 · in-silico training and the reality gap · FOptTrn 02 · interactive: three trainers

Precision

Gradient precision and FP8 E5M2
Training keeps activations and weights in BF16 or FP8 E4M3 and gradients in FP8 E5M2, which trades mantissa for range. Applying the inference series' noise rule, matching E5M2 needs about 5.3–5.8 ENOB and BF16 about 10.3–10.7, higher for spiky gradients whose crest factor is larger.Explained in: FOptTrn 02 · precision for training formats · · results.md §5 · the noise rule
Bit planes
An analogue engine with few bits of input resolution carries each integer as P binary planes, one optical pass per plane, recombined digitally with weights 2b. The read-out noise per plane is multiplied by √Σ4b (147.8 for 8 planes). Measured in a training loop, 4 planes cost about 0.12 in loss against 8 and 12 gained nothing.Explained in: FOptTrn 02 · precision for training formats · CiT results · ENOB and planes
Analogue noise in a training loop
Zero-mean read-out noise in the forward pass acts like injected noise, which training can tolerate or even exploit; noise in the gradient adds to minibatch noise. Biased errors (offset, nonlinearity, drift) do not average out and must be calibrated.Explained in: FOptTrn 02 · analogue noise in a training loop · FOptTrn 02 · interactive: three trainers

The training cluster

Layer-major scheduling
Passing every sequence of a step through one layer before moving to the next, so one mask write serves the whole step. It conflicts with pipeline parallelism and activation memory, which prefer sequence-major order.Explained in: FOptTrn 01 · layer-major order · FOptTrn 03 · layer-major scheduling against the pipeline
Error feedback
A compressor that keeps everything it did not send in a residual and adds it to the next step's input, so nothing is dropped, only delayed. In DeMo the delta compressed each step is the momentum plus this residual, which decays by 0.999 per step. What is subtracted must be what was actually sent: in the Compute in Transit experiment, feeding back the exact band instead of the band the emulated optics produced made the optics' errors a bias, and all six first-design runs diverged.Explained in: FOptTrn 03 · the optimizer step and DeMo · FOptTrn 02 · a measured case: optics inside DeMo’s feedback loop · CiT results · the feedback loop
Fixed frequency band
Sending the same transform coefficients (for example the lowest 1/16 of each chunk's DCT) every step instead of the largest ones (top-k): no comparisons, so a link module could compute it, and every worker's band lines up for summing. On a 15.8M-parameter GPT it ended at a validation loss of 3.124–3.158 against 1.785 for DeMo's top-k and 1.646 for AdamW.Explained in: FOptTrn 03 · a fixed band · CiT results · loss curves
Captured energy
The share of a tensor's energy (the sum of its squared transform coefficients) that a selector keeps. On the experiment's momentum with 2-D 64×64 chunks, top-k kept 0.643 and the fixed low band 0.013; a random set keeps about the keep fraction. It explains why the fixed band trains so much worse.Explained in: FOptTrn 03 · a fixed band · CiT results · captured energy
All-gather against all-reduce wire bytes
A ring all-reduce of B bytes sends 2(N−1)/N·B per worker whatever N is; a ring all-gather of P bytes per worker sends (N−1)·P, which grows with N. On 8 workers DeMo's 8× and 16× smaller payloads became only 2× and 4× fewer wire bytes. Summing a fixed band in the transform domain (it is linear) restores the all-reduce pattern.Explained in: FOptTrn 03 · the optimizer step and DeMo · FOptTrn 03 · where the band is computed · CiT results · bytes
Time and energy to a target loss
The measured steps to reach a loss times the step time (compute plus, for each sync round, latency and bytes over bandwidth), and the same steps times the energy per step. It weighs a compressor's saved bytes against the extra steps it needs: to 3.158 on 2 DCs × 4 over 100 GbE, AdamW takes 1.63 s and the fixed band 3.85 s.Explained in: FOptTrn 03 · training across data centres · CiT results · transit explorer
Cross-data-centre training and break-even bandwidth
Data-parallel workers split across data centres sync over a WAN far slower than the fabric inside one. The break-even bandwidth is the WAN rate below which one way of syncing reaches the target loss sooner than another, found by bisection: a band in a pluggable link module never breaks even with AdamW from 0.1 to 1,000 GB/s; summing the band at the switch wins below 1.57 GB/s.Explained in: FOptTrn 03 · where the band is computed · FOptTrn 03 · training across data centres · CiT results · break-even
DiLoCo-style sync
Workers train locally and sync only every H steps, dividing communication by H; DiLoCo (Douillard et al., 2023) also changes the optimizer. The Compute in Transit simulation's DiLoCo-style rows assume the steps to a loss are unchanged: an extrapolation, not a measurement. At H = 100 the bytes a band saves matter little.Explained in: FOptTrn 03 · training across data centres · CiT results · extrapolations
DeMo (decoupled momentum)
An optimizer that DCT-transforms the momentum in small chunks and sends only the largest coefficients between workers (Peng et al., 2024). Its transforms are about 5×10−6 of a step's FLOPs: what it saves is link bytes, not compute. Measured on a 15.8M-parameter GPT with 8 workers (Compute in Transit), top-k DeMo ended at 1.785 ± 0.007 against AdamW's 1.646 ± 0.005, with 8× less payload and 2× fewer wire bytes.Explained in: FOptTrn 03 · the optimizer step and DeMo · · results.md §3 · Compute in Transit
