Fourier Optics for Training

The companion to Fourier Optics for Inference: could a Fourier-optical engine accelerate LLM training? Training adds a backward pass, weights that change every step and gradients that need their own precision. Three decks work it out: where transforms appear in a training step, with an Amdahl analysis from a published script; optical backpropagation and in-situ training; and optical engines inside a training cluster.

The headline so far. Training does not rescue the transform share. For a Hyena-2 model of Llama-3-8B's shape at 4,096 tokens, transforms are 0.21% of a forward pass and 0.15% of a training step, because the backward pass has as many FFTs as the forward while the matmuls double. With block-circulant weights the step share is 12.2% (Amdahl bound 1.14x), but a fifth of that work multiplies two data-dependent spectra (the weight gradient) and cannot sit on a static mask, which leaves 9.7%. And the masks themselves change every step: on the series' illustrative 2-megapixel, 1 kHz device, rewriting them takes 0.5–2 s per step, comparable to or longer than the digital step. All numbers: analysis/results.md.

The backward pass

The 6N rule
Training a dense model costs about 6 FLOPs per parameter per token: 2 for the forward pass and 4 for the backward (the activation gradient and the weight gradient), plus an attention term that grows with sequence length. The analysis reproduces it exactly for the transformer variant.Planned: FOptTrn 01 · results.md §1
The gradient of a convolution is a correlation
For y = h ∗ u, the input gradient is G correlated with h, IFFT(G·conj(H)), and the filter gradient is G correlated with u, IFFT(G·conj(U)). A backward long convolution is one more FFT and two more Fourier-domain products than the forward; the analysis checks the formulas against direct sums and finite differences.Planned: FOptTrn 01 · results.md §6
Weight-masked and data-by-data products
A Fourier-domain product whose second operand is a weight spectrum (forward H, backward conj(H)) can be a mask written once per optimizer step. One whose operands both depend on the sample (conj(U)·G for the weight gradient) needs a new mask for every sequence, and in practice stays digital.Planned: FOptTrn 01 · results.md §2
Static share and the training Amdahl bound
The fraction of a training step's FLOPs that are transforms plus weight-masked products: what an engine with once-per-step masks could take. 1/(1−share) bounds the step speed-up. For Hyena-2 at 4,096 tokens it is 0.14%; for block-circulant weights 9.7%.Planned: FOptTrn 01 · results.md §2 · compare the inference bound
Mask rewrites per optimizer step
An inference engine writes its filter spectra once; a training engine must rewrite them after every optimizer step, for the forward and the backward pass. Processing the whole step layer by layer amortises each write over every sequence, but the frame budget still rivals the digital step time.Planned: FOptTrn 01, 03 · results.md §4 · compare mask capacity

Training the optics

Diffractive deep neural networks (D²NN)
Stacks of passive diffractive layers whose transmission is trained in simulation and then fabricated (Lin et al., Science 2018). Training is entirely digital; the optics only runs inference, so model mismatch after fabrication is the central problem.Planned: FOptTrn 02
In-situ backpropagation (the adjoint method)
In a reciprocal photonic circuit, the gradient with respect to every phase shifter can be measured by sending the error signal backwards and interfering it with the stored forward field (Hughes et al., Optica 2018; demonstrated in a programmable photonic chip by Pai et al., Science 2023). The hardware computes its own gradient.Planned: FOptTrn 02
Physics-aware training
Run the forward pass on the physical system and the backward pass through a differentiable digital model of it, so the update uses the real output but an approximate gradient (Wright et al., Nature 2022). It tolerates mismatch that pure in-silico training does not.Planned: FOptTrn 02
Direct feedback alignment (DFA)
Replace each layer's backpropagated error with a fixed random projection of the output error. The random projection is a large matrix multiply an optical scattering medium does for free; a photonic co-processor has trained networks this way (Launay et al., 2020). It scales poorly to transformers' accuracy.Planned: FOptTrn 02
The reality gap
The difference between the model a network was trained on and the hardware it runs on: fabrication error, drift, crosstalk, temperature. In-situ and hardware-in-the-loop training close it; in-silico training does not.Planned: FOptTrn 02

Precision

Gradient precision and FP8 E5M2
Training keeps activations and weights in BF16 or FP8 E4M3 and gradients in FP8 E5M2, which trades mantissa for range. Applying the inference series' noise rule, matching E5M2 needs about 5.3–5.8 ENOB and BF16 about 10.3–10.7, higher for spiky gradients whose crest factor is larger.Planned: FOptTrn 02 · results.md §5 · the noise rule
Analogue noise in a training loop
Zero-mean read-out noise in the forward pass acts like injected noise, which training can tolerate or even exploit; noise in the gradient adds to minibatch noise. Biased errors (offset, nonlinearity, drift) do not average out and must be calibrated.Planned: FOptTrn 02

The training cluster

Layer-major scheduling
Passing every sequence of a step through one layer before moving to the next, so one mask write serves the whole step. It conflicts with pipeline parallelism and activation memory, which prefer sequence-major order.Planned: FOptTrn 03
DeMo (decoupled momentum)
An optimizer that DCT-transforms the momentum in small chunks and sends only the largest coefficients between workers (Peng et al., 2024). Its transforms are about 5×10−6 of a step's FLOPs: what it saves is link bytes, not compute.Planned: FOptTrn 03 · results.md §3 · Compute in Transit
